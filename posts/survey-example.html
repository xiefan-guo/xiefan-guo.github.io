A Field Guide to Long Surveys: Test-Time Verification and Structured Search

Xiefan Guo · Published Nov 12, 2026 · Revised Nov 22, 2026 · 25 min read
Abstract Scaling inference-time computation has emerged as an orthogonal axis to pre-training scale for complex reasoning tasks. This survey organizes recent literature along two coupled dimensions: how partial reasoning states are scored (verification) and how compute budgets are allocated across candidate trajectories (search), with particular attention to horizon-dependent calibration drift.

1.Scope & Motivation

Standard autoregressive decoding commits irreversibly to every token it emits. For short factual recall, this limitation rarely surfaces; for multi-step mathematical proofs, program synthesis, or formal planning, a single unrecovered slip invalidates an otherwise promising trajectory.1

Test-time search addresses this fragility by separating proposal generation from selection. Given a fixed inference budget B, a policy proposes candidate partial trajectories while a verifier estimates the probability that a prefix leads to a valid solution. Understanding when this decomposition outperforms allocating the same budget to a larger base model is the thread unifying the works surveyed here.

2.Verifier Formulations

A verifier maps a problem specification x and a partial prefix y1:t to a scalar score st in [0, 1]. Despite wide architectural diversity across recent systems, nearly all learned verifiers instantiate one of two supervision regimes.

2.1Outcome vs. Process Supervision

Outcome Reward Models (ORMs) receive binary feedback only upon observing a complete solution, whereas Process Reward Models (PRMs) supervise intermediate steps directly or via Monte Carlo rollouts. While PRMs offer sharper credit assignment, their labels inherit the biases of the rollout policy used to estimate step-level value.

Definition 2.1 · Step-Level Value Consistency

For a fixed proposal policy pi and problem x, a process verifier V is martingale-consistent if, for every non-terminal prefix y1:t, the score V(x, y1:t) equals the expected next-step score Eyt+1 ~ pi[ V(x, y1:t+1) ].

2.2Calibration Under Horizon Shift

During search, verifiers are routinely queried on prefixes deeper than those seen during training. Empirically, discriminative verifiers exhibit over-confidence on long, stylistically fluent yet logically invalid chains.2 We decompose total ranking regret into calibration error and local discrimination error:

L(V; pi) = E[ (V(x, y1:t) − V(x, y1:t+1))2 ] + lambda · ECEdepth(V)
(1)

Penalizing depth-conditioned expected calibration error prevents best-first search from greedily expanding long dead-end branches solely because their scores drift upward with token count.

3.Taxonomy of Search Strategies

Table 1 summarizes four representative families of test-time search algorithms along verification granularity, compute scaling, and dominant failure modes.

Table 1: Comparative taxonomy of test-time search families under fixed compute budgets.
Method Family Verifier Signal Compute Scaling Primary Failure Mode
Best-of-N Outcome (terminal) Linear, parallel Reward hacking at large N
Step Beam Search Process (prefix) Width × Depth Premature diversity collapse
MCTS Variants Process + Rollouts Adaptive tree expansion High rollout latency
Verifier-Gated Revision Critique + Scalar Sequential refinement Self-confirmation loops

Two empirical patterns recur across independent evaluations:

  • Diversity dominates small budgets. Under 16 samples, independent parallel sampling with lightweight reranking is difficult to beat.
  • Adaptive search wins on hard tails. Where pass@1 falls below 5%, calibrated step-level pruning reallocates compute away from broken prefixes and yields substantial gains over Best-of-N.

4.Open Problems

Looking ahead, three bottlenecks appear especially fertile: (i) hybrid symbolic-neural verification invoking deterministic checkers whenever formal subgoals can be extracted; (ii) amortizing search back into the policy without collapsing exploration entropy; and (iii) principled stopping rules that terminate early on easy instances so compute is spent where genuine uncertainty resides.
