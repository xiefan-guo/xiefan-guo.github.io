Reward models help evaluate, select, and improve generated images, edits, videos, and world-model rollouts. This post compares them along five axes: construction and interface, supervision and objective, learning workflow, feedback granularity, and application scope. It also examines explicit rubrics and training-free rewards, then compares benchmarks by task, annotation unit, and evaluation dimension.

This post covers selected work available through 28 October 2026. Model families, successor versions, and benchmark papers are cross-checked against primary methods and author-maintained releases; material paper/release differences are identified below.

1. Why visual rewards need a closer look

A visually appealing output can still omit a requested object, alter an unrelated identity, ignore an action, or execute the wrong camera command. A useful reward must specify both the judgment being made and the visual evidence needed to support it.

Three widely used descriptions—Bradley–Terry preference learning (BT), generative reward modeling (GRM), and pointwise scoring—do not define mutually exclusive model families. BT specifies a likelihood over relative utilities. GRM specifies a token-generating judgment mechanism. “Pointwise” can describe either one-artifact inference or supervision by individual ratings. Treating these as sibling categories obscures methods that combine them. We instead describe each method along five axes and organize the concrete applications by domain → generation or editing.

The reward estimator and its consuming optimizer are separate: a regression-trained scorer can guide generator RL, a judge can itself undergo RL, and direct preference optimization can train a generator without a separate reward model.

1.1 What this post covers

The scope includes rewards for visual generation and editing, with visual-quality models, frozen perception, geometry, and predictive priors identified as supporting foundations or reward constructions. Visual program synthesis is included where intermediate renders clarify process feedback.

“Generation” includes text-conditioned synthesis, image-conditioned video, and controlled future rollouts. “Editing” requires a source, a requested transformation, and preservation requirements: an initial video frame or camera command alone does not establish editing. Task coverage follows the cited experiments; corpus sizes retain their units, versions, and train/test roles.

2. A multi-axis framework

Let $c$ be the full task condition and $y$ a candidate output from generator $g_\phi(y\mid c)$. For image editing, $c=(x,u)$ contains the source image and edit instruction. Video editing additionally requires source frames and temporal correspondence. Controlled world generation conditions on observation history and actions. The observation operator $\mathcal O(c,Y)$ selects the pixels, frames, latent states, or tool results shown to the evaluator for candidate set $Y$.

A discriminative estimator produces a scalar $r_\theta(c,y)$, a criterion vector $\mathbf r_\theta(c,y)$, or a score distribution. An autoregressive evaluator predicts $p_\theta(z,d\mid\mathcal O(c,Y))$, where $z$ is an optional assessment and $d$ a rating or verdict. A hybrid may generate $z$ and predict a continuous reward from a separate head. A comparator $J_\theta(c,y_a,y_b)$ need not admit a globally consistent scalar utility.

2.1 Worked classifications

HPSv3[1] uses a discriminative distributional head, pairwise preference likelihood, supervised fitting, and whole-image labels for image generation; it nevertheless scores one image at inference. TRM[2] generates a rubric and pointwise scores, uses supervised targets followed by pair-conditioned evaluator RL, and covers image generation and editing. DeScore[3] combines generated reasoning with a scalar head, evaluator GRPO, and auxiliary BT ranking for video.

Label topology and loss are distinct. A pair can supervise a BT utility difference, an A/B/Tie classifier, or an autoregressive verdict through token cross-entropy. Complete rankings can be reduced to pairs or optimized jointly.

3. Supervision topology and learning objectives

3.1 Pointwise ratings, categories, and criterion targets

For an individual target score $s$, direct regression minimizes $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{reg}}=\mathbb E[(r_\theta(c,y)-s)^2]$. Multi-criterion supervision replaces the scalar with labeled coordinates and a mask for missing targets. VideoScore[4] uses a five-output regression variant. RAHF[5] combines score and spatial heatmap regression with token-sequence prediction; its local targets contain information absent from an overall preference.

A categorical model instead predicts $p_\theta(k\mid c,y)$ over rating levels. A continuous score can be recovered by $\hat s=\sum_{k=1}^{K}v_kp_\theta(k\mid c,y)$ for declared level values $v_k$. Q-Align[6] learns text-defined quality levels using token cross-entropy and computes an expected-level score. Its ordered target words do not enforce an explicit ordinal loss. CheckDistill[7] predicts criterion-wise score distributions after distillation. Neither a numeric output nor a distribution proves absolute calibration across prompts, raters, or generator families.

3.2 Pairwise utility learning and direct comparison

For candidates under a shared condition, the standard BT model is

$$ P_\theta(y_a\succ y_b\mid c)=\sigma\!\left(\frac{r_\theta(c,y_a)-r_\theta(c,y_b)}{\tau}\right),\qquad \tau>0. $$

$$ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{BT}}=-\mathbb E_{(c,y_w,y_l)}\log P_\theta(y_w\succ y_l\mid c). $$

Two-candidate softmax gives the same binary likelihood. PickScore[8] and HPS v2[9] fit preference targets with softmax/KL; ImageReward[10] expands human rankings into comparison pairs. Soft tie targets, explicit A/Tie/B likelihoods, and integration over predicted score distributions are distinct extensions. VideoReward[11] models ties; HPSv3 and EditReward[12] use distribution-aware ranking. BT identifies relative utility within a condition, not its additive offset. Reward scale affects calibration and generator updates even when the ranking is unchanged.

A direct generative comparator predicts a verdict from both candidates. RewardDance[13] uses the probability of a “yes” token in a reference-comparative judgment. The reference and instruction are part of the scoring condition. This is different from assuming that comparison logits equal the difference of two independently computed utilities. Its separate pointwise generative variant combines BT with an auxiliary CE loss—an explicit example of generative scoring and pairwise utility training coexisting.

3.3 Listwise supervision: preserve candidate-set structure

Listwise learning uses the joint ordering or relevance structure of a candidate set. A standard full-ranking likelihood is the Plackett–Luce form, for permutation $\pi$ and utilities $r_i=r_\theta(c,y_i)$:

$$ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{PL}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}\log\frac{\exp(r_{\pi_j}/\tau)}{\sum_{l=j}^{K}\exp(r_{\pi_l}/\tau)}. $$

This is one listwise formulation, not a synonym for all listwise losses. Expanding a human ranking into BT pairs remains pairwise training. It also creates dependent examples: a group of $K$ candidates does not supply $K(K-1)/2$ independent annotation events.

Probe-Select[14] trains a small probe on intermediate diffusion features using final-image teacher scores, softmax-based listwise ranking, and prompt contrastive alignment. Its loss differs from the Plackett–Luce formulation above; §7.2 distinguishes this early outcome prediction from process supervision.

3.4 Judgment-sequence supervision

A generative evaluator can learn ratings, comparisons, criterion answers, or critiques with the same autoregressive objective. If $w=(z,d)$ is the target assessment and $o=\mathcal O(c,Y)$, then

$$ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{SFT}}=-\mathbb E\sum_t\log p_\theta(w_t\mid o,w_{<t}). $$

UnifiedReward[15] unifies heterogeneous evaluation tasks through answer-token supervision. RationalRewards[16] trains on preference-anchored rationales, projected pointwise ratings, and comparisons. WorldReward[17] learns action-grounded reasoning and A/B/Tie judgments. These models may consume preference labels without fitting a BT scalar utility. The target sequence, its teacher, and the mask applied to training tokens determine what the objective actually teaches.

Pointwise/pairwise/listwise describe relationships among evaluated artifacts; token CE describes a prediction loss. Their interaction must remain explicit. The same applies to criterion-conditioned MPS[18]: each criterion yields a per-image score, but learning uses pairwise preference distributions.

3.5 Vector comparisons, probit preferences, and energy objectives

Scalar BT is not the only comparative model. LLaVA-Reward[19] also fits a General Preference Model (GPM): vector representations are compared through a skew-symmetric bilinear logit, rather than subtracting two scalar utilities. With $A^\top=-A$, one can write $\ell(a,b)=\langle Av_a,v_b\rangle/\tau$ and minimize $-\log\sigma(\ell(w,l))$. Such comparisons can express nontransitive preferences. GPM is a preference representation, whereas GRM describes a generated judgment interface.

SURE-LRM[20] uses a Gaussian/Thurstone link:

$$P(w\succ l)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{\mu_w-\mu_l}{\sqrt{v_w+v_l}}\right),$$

where $\Phi$ is the Gaussian CDF. Its probit negative log-likelihood differs from logistic BT. DiNa-LRM[21] uses a timestep-calibrated probit relation with a fidelity loss instead of NLL. The probability link, target topology, and optimized loss should therefore all be recorded. Variance learned from preferences is not automatically calibrated uncertainty over individual raters.

GT-SVJ[22] adds contrastive energy pretraining on real/generated/perturbed video, followed by human-score MSE and BT aggregation. This differs from adversarial co-training with a changing generator. Aligning Human Sense[23] combines calibrated score regression and Wasserstein matching; its point-mass targets should not be mistaken for empirical distributions of all rater opinions.

4.1 Reinforcing or preference-tuning the evaluator

In evaluator RL, the sampled action is a judgment or reasoning trajectory, not an image. Schematically,

$$ \max_\theta\ \mathbb E_{w\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid o)}[R_{\mathrm{judge}}(w;\ell)]-\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_\theta\Vert p_{\mathrm{ref}}), $$

where $\ell$ is the available supervisory evidence. UnifiedReward-Think[24] combines distilled cold-start SFT, rejection-sampling SFT, and GRPO. VideoScore2[25] reinforces score accuracy and format. VR-Thinker[26] also rewards evidence acquisition through frame retrieval. SpatialCritic[27] uses verifiable spatial preferences and a cost term. Correct final labels are weaker evidence than faithful intermediate reasoning; tool use should be evaluated against equal observation budgets.

UnifiedReward-Flex[28] instead follows structured reasoning SFT with DPO on evaluator responses. In this setting DPO changes the judge. The same acronym in Diffusion-DPO changes the visual generator. TRM’s pairwise dual-group GRPO trains generated pointwise scores with a margin-saturation mechanism; paired supervision does not turn that policy-gradient update into BT.

4.2 Distillation, self-training, and compression

Distillation transfers teacher behavior through supervised targets or distribution matching. Its training signal may be a rationale, a score distribution, or a teacher with privileged inputs. CheckDistill uses checklist-equipped teacher supervision and reliability weighting to train a student that directly scores an edit without emitting the checklist. A generic distribution-matching objective is

$$ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{KD}}=\mathbb E\!\left[w(o)\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q_T(\cdot\mid o,e)\,\Vert\,p_\theta(\cdot\mid o)\right)\right], $$

where $e$ denotes privileged evidence and $w(o)$ a reliability weight. This equation summarizes the design space, rather than specifying every implementation. Teacher rationale SFT and online self-distillation are different workflows. Neither creates independent ground truth, and filtering for teacher–label agreement can discard difficult or disputed cases.

4.3 Adversarially learned rewards

Adv-GRPO[29] learns a reward discriminator while the image generator changes. For a binary reference-versus-generated discriminator, the basic objective is

$$ \mathcal L_D=-\mathbb E_{y\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\log D_\theta(y)-\mathbb E_{y\sim g_\phi}\log(1-D_\theta(y)). $$

Adv-GRPO alternates discriminator learning and generator GRPO. Its implementations include a BCE preference-model adaptation and a frozen-DINO feature branch with global/patch classification and hinge loss; the latter is not described by the BCE equation verbatim. The changing reference-versus-policy signal requires fixed external evaluation.

4.4 Continual learning, co-adaptation, and context updates

HPSv3++[30] adds a different workflow from evaluator RL. After backbone migration, its first stage projects conflicting new-data gradients away from an HPDv3 reference gradient. The second combines labeled preference fitting with unlabeled rollout-group objectives and capability/iteration conditioning. Increased score dispersion is an adaptation signal, not proof of better human calibration. The reward model learns from recorded generator rollouts; this should not be described as GRPO training of the evaluator.

RetouchIQ[31] instead alternates reward-policy updates using progressively harder policy-produced edits. RewardHarness[32] changes a library of scoring instructions while keeping model weights frozen. These are parameter co-adaptation and context adaptation, respectively; neither should be collapsed into adversarial real/fake discrimination.

4.5 Label provenance and splitting

Human ratings, VLM annotations, distilled rationales, controlled corruptions, and executable checks should be reported separately. AI feedback can expand coverage but inherits the teacher’s blind spots. Constructed interventions can isolate a failure only when other properties remain controlled. For all workflows, split by independent prompt, source image, trajectory, or generator as appropriate; randomly splitting expanded pairs can leak shared candidates into evaluation.

6. Rubrics: making the scoring criteria explicit

A rubric specifies criteria, score-level descriptions, and possibly priorities or mandatory constraints; a checklist is its binary special case. A multi-output head alone is not an explicit rubric, and a yes/no answer is not automatically verifiable. The judge, rubric, or aggregation can each be fixed or learned.

Two language-side foundations clarify the decomposition. LLM-Rubric[59] combines rubric-conditioned answer distributions through learned human-rating calibration. Rubrics as Rewards[60] generates task-specific criteria and uses rubric-conditioned feedback to train a response policy with GRPO. Neither establishes visual-generation performance; their language datasets are excluded from the visual inventory.

6.1 Criteria, judgments, and aggregation are separate components

Write a rubric as $\rho=G_\eta(c,e)$, where $e$ may contain a fixed schema, seed preferences, source evidence, or candidate audits. A criterion evaluator produces $s_k=J_\theta(\mathcal O(c,y);\rho_k)$, and an aggregator returns $R_\rho=A_\omega(s_1,\ldots,s_K)$. The three components need not all be learned. After mapping item scores to a common range, a common aggregation is

$$R_\rho(c,y)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_k s_k}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_k},\qquad w_k\geq0,\quad \sum_k w_k>0.$$

Normalized weights preserve the shared score range but do not equalize criterion difficulty or calibrate scores across prompts. Mandatory constraints may gate eligibility or cap a score. When candidate inspection informs criterion construction, all compared candidates should face the same checked rubric.

7.3 Training-free reward extraction

Training-free must name the stage. Reward extraction introduces no new parameter fitting of the evaluator or its aggregation; pretraining remains part of its provenance. Intrinsic self-rewards may have no dedicated RM objective yet change through shared-model updates. The generator, inference candidates, or textual rubric may still be optimized; §7.4 identifies partly fitted boundary cases.

The main constructions are embedding similarity, conditional token likelihood, atomic question verification, prompted ratings/rubrics, and predictive or geometric consistency. These describe reward computation rather than learning objectives.

7.3.1 Read It Back: extracting a reward from prompt likelihood

Read It Back[77] introduces SpectraReward: a frozen MLLM scores an image by the mean log-likelihood of the original prompt under teacher forcing. One forward pass yields token likelihoods; no caption decoding, sampled judgment, preference labels, or reward fine-tuning is required. For prompt $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_T)$ and image $y$,

$$R_{\mathcal M}(x,y)=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\log p_{\mathcal M}(x_{t+1}\mid x_{\leq t},y).$$

Self-SpectraReward reuses a unified model’s understanding branch. This removes a separate external reward model while the generator still undergoes RL. A text-only likelihood correction cancels within a same-prompt relative-advantage group; this does not establish calibration across prompts.

PromptEcho[78] uses a closely related negative caption-token CE with an explicit descriptive query. GvU[79] instead exponentiates mean log-likelihood into a geometric-mean probability. For the same conditional token probabilities, mean log-likelihood and its exponential induce the same ranking; changing the descriptive query can change that ranking. The nonlinear transformation also generally changes normalized advantages, so these are not numerically identical training signals.

9.5 Avoid leakage and incomparable averages

Report uncertainty by resampling independent prompt or source groups rather than treating every expanded comparison as independent. Provide macro averages over tasks/generators alongside sample-weighted micro averages; a large easy category can otherwise dominate. The bootstrap unit, number of independent groups, inter-annotator agreement, exclusion rules, and tie prevalence belong with the main results.

For video, publish both nominal clip length and the frames observed. For critique-driven editing, fix the editor, revision rounds, and compute budget. For world models, preserve the same starting state and actions. Selection comparisons use identical candidate pools and an independent evaluator.

A useful robustness audit swaps candidate order, perturbs irrelevant formatting, changes the generator family, and introduces close visual near-misses. Text embedded in an image or video is content to evaluate, not authority to redefine the rubric. Evaluators should also be tested on locally correct but globally inconsistent outputs and on polished outputs that violate mandatory constraints.

10. Using rewards to improve generators

With a fixed reward, a common conceptual objective is

$$\max_\phi\,\mathbb E_{c,y\sim g_\phi}[r_\theta(c,y)]-\beta\,\mathbb E_c D_{\mathrm{KL}}(g_\phi(\cdot\mid c)\Vert g_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid c)). $$

This conceptual regularizer limits departure from a reference distribution; its implementation depends on the generator and is not the exact loss of every method below.

11. Open problems and research directions

11.1 Identifiable criteria and non-compensatory requirements

Correlated criterion labels do not identify independent causes of preference. Use controlled counterexamples to compare scalar ranking, weighted criteria, and feasibility-first selection on the same candidates, measuring successful edits, unintended changes, and false rejections together.

11.2 Grounded explanations and useful repairs

Verify explanations against regions, frames, or executable checks, then test whether following the critique repairs the defect without damaging correct content. Distilled direct scorers need stress tests for distinctions previously exposed by teacher reasoning.

11.3 Adaptive observation and long-horizon evaluation

Compare uniform and adaptive observation at equal cost on clips with controlled event duration and location. World-model tests additionally need branching actions, revisits, and hidden-state consequences.

11.4 Personalization, disagreement, and uncertainty

Test personalization with repeated feedback, held-out users, and a declared adaptation budget. Preserve ambiguous preferences rather than forcing a universal winner, and calibrate uncertainty before using it for abstention or routing.

PIGReward’s history-conditioned judgments and PAM∃LA’s user-conditioned ratings provide concrete tests: evaluate adaptation from rated context separately from zero-shot new-user generalization.

11.5 Robustness under optimization and changing generators

Compare annotation budgets spent on random examples, uncertain pairs, and high-reward failures from the current generator. Maintain a fixed external audit set and unseen-generator tests; release rubrics, preprocessing, aggregation, tie rules, sampling budgets, and split definitions alongside checkpoints.

11.6 Filling the editing branches without assuming transfer

Rendered video edits need supervision for temporal propagation and preservation. Persistent-world editing additionally needs state invariants and tests of action-dependent consequences; these remain gaps beyond the current rollout and editing-plan evaluations.
